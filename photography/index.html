01 / TIME

Shutter speed

How long you collect light.

1/1000 second is a very short exposure. 1/15 second is much longer. While the sensor collects light, anything that moves can leave a trail.

Faster · 1/1000 s
Freezes movement, but collects less light.
Slower · 1/15 s
Collects more light, but movement blurs. Even your hands can move the camera.

Try this ↙ Move toward 1/15 s. Watch the cyclist blur while the buildings stay still.

On your iPhone Open Camera Controls → Shutter Speed. Stabilization helps with hand shake; it cannot freeze a moving person. [1]

02 / OPENING

Aperture

The opening light passes through.

An f-number is focal length divided by the opening’s diameter. A smaller number means a wider opening. Think of a wide doorway letting more light in.

Wide · f/1.48
More light and a shallower zone of sharpness.
Narrow · f/4
Less light and a deeper zone of sharpness. With shutter and ISO fixed, f/4 is about 2.9 stops darker than f/1.48.

Try this ↙ Keep the flowers in focus. Close the aperture and watch the houses become clearer.

On your iPhone The 18 Pro’s Main camera has f/1.48, f/1.8, f/2.8 and f/4 settings. Portrait Depth Control instead simulates blur with processing. Small phone sensors have more depth of field than large cameras at the same f-number and framing. [1, 2]

03 / SIGNAL

ISO

How the captured signal is scaled.

Raising ISO makes the rendered image brighter at the same aperture and shutter speed. It does not bring more light into the sensor.

Low · ISO 100
Useful when there is enough light. Usually offers more highlight headroom.
High · ISO 3200
Useful when a fast shutter matters in low light. Low-light photos often look grainier because fewer photons were captured; ISO alone is not the whole cause.

Try this ↙ Increase ISO. In this equal-brightness example, the simulated camera shortens the shutter to compensate: less light, more visible noise.

On your iPhone Auto ISO is a useful default. For direct ISO control, use a camera app that exposes it; Apple’s Pro tools guide lists aperture, shutter and white balance. Prefer a sharp, noisy photo to an accidentally blurred one. [1, 3]

04 / THE BALANCING ACT

Exposure

Three controls. Different tradeoffs.

Aperture and shutter determine the light captured. ISO affects how bright that capture is rendered. The same brightness can come from very different combinations.

One stop = twice or half
1/250 → 1/125 s doubles captured light. ISO 100 → 200 doubles the nominal brightness. f/2.8 → f/4 roughly halves the light.
Read the histogram
Left = dark pixels; right = bright pixels. Piling up against an edge can mean lost detail. A bright or dark scene does not need a centered histogram.

Try this ↙ Start at f/2.8, 1/250 s, ISO 100. Change to 1/500 s, then ISO 200. Brightness returns; motion looks sharper.

On your iPhone Exposure compensation asks auto exposure for a brighter (+EV) or darker (−EV) result. It is different from manually fixing all three settings. Computational HDR may combine several captures. [3, 4]

05 / COLOR

White balance

Tell the camera what neutral looks like.

A white wall looks orange under warm bulbs and blue in shade. White balance compensates for the light’s color so neutral objects look neutral again.

Lower setting · 3200 K
Compensates for warm indoor light by cooling the image.
Higher setting · 7500 K
Compensates for blue shade by warming the image. Tint is a separate green–magenta correction.

Try this ↙ This scene is lit by 5500 K daylight. Match it for neutral whites, then deliberately make the scene cooler or warmer.

On your iPhone Camera Controls → White Balance offers manual choices. This Kelvin slider teaches the principle; it does not reproduce the phone’s exact interface. Mixed lighting may need a compromise. [1, 5]

06 / FRAMING

Focal length

How much of the world fits in the frame.

Short focal lengths give a wide view. Long ones give a narrow view and make distant details fill more of the image. Phone specs use 35 mm equivalent focal lengths so cameras can be compared.

Wide · 13–24 mm equivalent
More surroundings. Useful for buildings, interiors and environmental portraits.
Long · 100–200 mm equivalent
A tighter frame. Useful for distant details. Camera movement becomes more noticeable.

Try this ↙ Zoom in from the same position. Framing changes, but perspective does not. The “compressed” look comes from moving farther away to keep your subject the same size.

On your iPhone 13 mm, 24 mm and 100 mm correspond to Ultra Wide, Main and Telephoto. The 48 mm and 200 mm views use sensor crops, marketed as “optical-quality.” They are not extra lenses. [2, 6]

07 / ATTENTION

Focus & depth of field

Choose what deserves to be sharp.

Focus sets the distance of greatest sharpness. Depth of field is the zone around that distance that looks acceptably sharp. They are related, but different.

Focus distance
Switch between the nearby flowers and the distant houses. Autofocus chooses this for you; tapping a subject lets you choose.
Depth of field
Aperture, subject distance, focal length, sensor size and viewing conditions affect perceived sharpness. Getting close can make background blur more obvious.

Try this ↙ Focus on the flowers, then the houses. Watch the sharp plane move through the scene.

On your iPhone Tap your subject to focus; touch and hold to lock focus and exposure. Portrait mode uses a depth estimate to add blur, so inspect fine edges such as hair. [4, 6]

03 / THE REST OF THE LANGUAGE

Beyond the sliders.

“Pro” is a collection of tools.
Use the ones the picture needs.

01 RAW, ProRAW & HEIF +

HEIF and JPEG are compact, processed pictures ready to share. RAW keeps more capture information for editing. Apple ProRAW combines RAW data with Apple’s processing; it is not an unprocessed sensor dump. Files are larger, and it will not rescue every blown highlight. Start with HEIF; choose ProRAW for photos you intend to edit. [7]

02 HDR & dynamic range +

Dynamic range is the span from useful shadow detail to useful highlight detail. HDR processing combines or maps tones to fit more of a contrasty scene into the result. Capture HDR and an HDR display are different things. Try a window portrait: expose for the face, then for the view outside, and notice the compromise.

03 Megapixels & resolution +

A megapixel is one million image pixels. More pixels can help with cropping or large prints when the lens, light and technique supply enough detail. They do not guarantee a sharper photograph. Start at the default resolution; try a higher resolution in good light with a still subject and compare fine detail. [8]

04 Optical & digital zoom +

A different physical lens changes the optical view. A sensor crop uses part of an existing sensor. Further digital zoom enlarges or reconstructs that smaller view. “Optical-quality” is a quality claim, not a continuously zooming lens. In dim light a phone may choose another camera and crop it. Compare textures at several zoom settings. [2]

05 Stabilization & Night mode +

Optical stabilization moves lens elements or the sensor to counter camera shake. Night mode can combine multiple exposures to improve a dark scene. Both help with still subjects; movement can still cause blur or ghosting. Brace your elbows, lean on a wall, and ask a night portrait subject to hold still.

06 Macro & minimum focus distance +

Every lens has a closest distance at which it can focus. Moving closer than that makes the subject blurry, even if you tap it. Macro lets you focus very close for small details. If the phone switches cameras as you approach a flower, its macro system may be taking over. Move back a little if focus struggles. [2]

07 Bokeh & Portrait mode +

Depth of field describes the sharp zone; bokeh describes the appearance of the out-of-focus areas. Portrait mode adds blur using an estimated depth map. The displayed f-number in a depth effect is a rendering control, distinct from a physical lens aperture. Try putting more space between your subject and the background.

08 FPS & video shutter speed +

Frame rate is pictures recorded per second. Shutter speed is the exposure time of each picture. At 30 fps, 1/60 s is a common starting point for natural motion blur; it is a creative convention, not a rule. A faster shutter looks crisper or choppier. In bright light an ND filter can help maintain a slower shutter.

09 ProRes, Log & color grading +

ProRes is a family of video recording formats suited to editing, usually with large files. Log is a tone encoding designed to preserve flexibility for color grading; it can look flat before a display transform is applied. Neither makes a video cinematic by itself. Use ordinary video first; choose these when your editing workflow needs them. [2]

10 Composition & the light itself +

Settings cannot choose the photograph for you. Notice the direction and softness of light, simplify the background, and check the edges of the frame. The rule of thirds is a starting point, not a requirement. Photograph the same subject from three positions before changing a setting.

04 / TAKE IT OUTSIDE

Your first ten minutes.

  1. Start in auto. Clean the lens. Find window light and a still subject.
  2. Choose attention. Tap to focus. Adjust exposure and watch bright areas.
  3. Change one thing. Photograph movement with two shutter speeds. Compare.
  4. Look at the result. Is the subject sharp? Are the colors intentional? Does the framing help?

On iPhone 18 Pro, open Camera Controls for Lens Aperture, Shutter Speed, White Balance and Histogram. The Main camera’s aperture is adjustable; other cameras have their own lens limits. Auto remains a useful creative tool. [1, 2]
