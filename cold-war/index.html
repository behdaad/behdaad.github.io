1945 · 2 historical events

The atomic age begins

The Trinity test proves a nuclear weapon can work. The United States then drops atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki. The weapons emerge from World War II, but their destructive power reshapes the postwar rivalry. The Atomic Cafe opens with this history before following the public messages used to make nuclear danger seem manageable.

Allies face the peace

Victory over Nazi Germany leaves the United States, Soviet Union, Britain and France occupying Germany. At Potsdam, disagreements over reparations, borders and Europe's future expose tensions inside the wartime alliance. The Cold War emerges through the following years rather than beginning with a single declaration. The Third Man offers a later view of occupied Vienna, not a reconstruction of this conference.

Containment and reconstruction

The Truman Doctrine promises American support to Greece and Turkey, framing their crises as part of a wider struggle. The Marshall Plan follows with economic aid for European recovery. Soviet control in Eastern Europe and competing ideas about security and political order harden the division. Neither policy is directly dramatised by the selected dramas.

Life between occupation zones

Vienna, like Berlin, is divided among the four occupying powers, but Austria's later path will differ from Germany's. The Third Man follows an invented criminal investigation through the city's ruins, shortages and black market. Its setting makes the uneasy postwar order tangible; Harry Lime's story is fiction, not a documented espionage operation.

Berlin survives a blockade

The Soviet blockade cuts land access to the Western sectors of Berlin. Western aircraft supply the city until the blockade is lifted in May 1949. This confrontation helps turn the occupation of Germany into a lasting division. The Third Man is set in Vienna and does not depict the Berlin airlift.

Context stop: shared occupation geography does not make the film a depiction of Berlin.

1949 · 2 historical events

Alliances form; China changes course

NATO is founded as a collective-defence alliance. In China, Communist victory establishes the People's Republic while the Nationalist government retreats to Taiwan. The Soviet-led Warsaw Pact follows in 1955. These developments widen the rivalry, but communist states do not remain a single harmonious bloc: China and the Soviet Union will become bitter rivals.

Context stop: none of the films explains the Chinese Civil War or the later Sino-Soviet split.

The American nuclear monopoly ends

The Soviet Union tests its first atomic device at Semipalatinsk in present-day Kazakhstan. The prospect of nuclear attack now works in both directions, accelerating competition over weapons, delivery systems and civil defence. The Atomic Cafe supplies the American culture of fear and reassurance around this rivalry; it is not an inside account of the Soviet programme.

The Cold War becomes a hot war

North Korea invades South Korea. A US-led United Nations force intervenes, and China later enters on the North's side. The fighting devastates the peninsula; an armistice in 1953 stops major hostilities without a peace treaty. Calling this a proxy war must not erase Korean aims, decisions or suffering.

Hydrogen bombs raise the stakes

The US Ivy Mike test demonstrates a thermonuclear device in the Marshall Islands. The arms race moves beyond the first atomic bombs towards vastly larger explosive yields, while testing exposes Pacific communities and other populations to fallout. The Atomic Cafe examines the reassuring public language of this era; its archive collage should be read critically, not as safety advice.

The French defeat at Dien Bien Phu leads into the Geneva settlement, which provides for a temporary military division of Vietnam and envisages elections. The intended political settlement fails to reunify the country. Decisions at the nuclear brink

Discovery of Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba produces a confrontation over inspections, a US naval quarantine and the risk of invasion. A settlement removes Soviet missiles from Cuba, includes an American pledge not to invade, and secretly provides for US missile removal from Turkey. Thirteen Days follows the American decision rooms; Cuban and Soviet perspectives need the accompanying history.

A limit on testing, not an end to the race

The United States, Soviet Union and United Kingdom sign the Limited Test Ban Treaty, prohibiting tests in the atmosphere, outer space and underwater. Underground testing continues. The agreement helps limit radioactive fallout and establishes an arms-control precedent; it does not abolish nuclear weapons or halt their development.

Context stop: The Atomic Cafe focuses on earlier atomic-age culture; no selected title reconstructs this treaty negotiation.

America escalates in Vietnam

The Gulf of Tonkin resolution gives President Johnson broad authority to expand US military action; large-scale bombing and American combat deployments follow. Vietnam's struggle also grows from colonial rule, partition and rival Vietnamese political projects. Read this before Full Metal Jacket and Platoon: their later combat stories do not explain how the war began.

Communist forces launch attacks across South Vietnam during the Tet holiday. US and South Vietnamese forces retake lost ground, but the scale and visibility of the offensive damage American claims of progress. Trying to keep the nuclear club from growing

The Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty opens for signature. It combines commitments to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons with peaceful nuclear cooperation and negotiations towards disarmament. Preventing new nuclear-armed states and reducing existing arsenals are related but different tasks; the Cold War arms race continues under this new framework.

Context stop: sharing the year with the Vietnam films is not evidence that they depict nuclear diplomacy.

The Prague Spring is crushed

Alexander Dubček's reforms in Czechoslovakia raise hopes for a less repressive socialist system. A Soviet-led Warsaw Pact invasion ends the experiment. The Cold War includes conflicts within the communist world as well as confrontation across its borders; events in Prague are distinct from the simultaneous war in Vietnam.

Negotiation alongside competition

Nixon visits China and signs the SALT I agreements with the Soviet Union: limits on strategic missile launchers and the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty. Limiting defences is tied to deterrence, not a promise that nuclear war is safe. The opening to Beijing exploits the Sino-Soviet split. Détente reduces some risks without ending rivalry or wars elsewhere; the 1975 Helsinki agreements later connect European security with human-rights commitments.

Context stop: no selected film directly covers these summits or agreements.

A maintenance accident at a Titan II silo near Damascus, Arkansas, causes a fuel leak and a missile explosion. One airman dies; the nuclear warhead does not detonate. Reform and a different nuclear conversation

Mikhail Gorbachev takes power in 1985 and pursues economic restructuring and greater openness. Summits with Ronald Reagan lead to the 1987 Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty, eliminating a whole category of missiles. Reform and arms control change the possibilities, but the Soviet Union's eventual dissolution is not a settled plan.

One Germany, an uneven transition

Germany reunites after negotiations between the two German states and the four former occupying powers. Institutions, employment and everyday consumer life change quickly in the East. Good Bye Lenin! makes the disorientation personal, mixing affection, loss and satire; its family story is not a complete verdict on life under the GDR.

An East Berlin son tries to protect his recovering mother from a shock by keeping the old GDR alive inside their flat while the world outside changes. End the route with comedy, grief and the confusion of sudden freedom.

The rest of the picture

This is an introduction through eight dramas and two documentaries, weighted towards Europe and American viewpoints. Vietnam now includes the French colonial war and later American infantry experience; nuclear coverage includes propaganda, testing, arms-control milestones and arsenal safety. These are complementary perspectives, not a complete account of either subject. Cold War rivalry intersected with local struggles, decolonisation and civil wars; those societies had their own aims. Context stops cover China, Korea, Bandung, Hungary, Prague, Chile and Afghanistan, but no selected film directly tells those stories. Vietnamese, Soviet, Pacific Islander and other civilian perspectives still deserve more attention, as do the Sino-Soviet split, the space race and many interventions. Blank years are gaps in this guide, not years when nothing happened.

The Berlin Wall opened in November 1989, Germany reunited in October 1990, and the Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991. These were distinct developments, not one event. This guide ends in 1991, a conventional endpoint for the Cold War; nuclear arsenals, political divisions and the consequences of its wars remained. Films open a route into the history; the linked historical sources supply the wider picture.
