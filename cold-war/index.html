1945 · 1 historical event

Allies face the peace

Victory over Nazi Germany leaves the United States, Soviet Union, Britain and France occupying Germany. At Potsdam, disagreements over reparations, borders and Europe's future expose tensions inside the wartime alliance. The Cold War emerges through the following years rather than beginning with a single declaration. The Third Man offers a later view of occupied Vienna, not a reconstruction of this conference.

Containment and reconstruction

The Truman Doctrine promises American support to Greece and Turkey, framing their crises as part of a wider struggle. The Marshall Plan follows with economic aid for European recovery. Soviet control in Eastern Europe and competing ideas about security and political order harden the division. Neither policy is directly dramatised by these six films.

Context stop: the opening of this rivalry needs its own history, beyond the atmosphere of the first film.

Life between occupation zones

Vienna, like Berlin, is divided among the four occupying powers, but Austria's later path will differ from Germany's. The Third Man follows an invented criminal investigation through the city's ruins, shortages and black market. Its setting makes the uneasy postwar order tangible; Harry Lime's story is fiction, not a documented espionage operation.

Berlin survives a blockade

The Soviet blockade cuts land access to the Western sectors of Berlin. Western aircraft supply the city until the blockade is lifted in May 1949. This confrontation helps turn the occupation of Germany into a lasting division. The Third Man is set in Vienna and does not depict the Berlin airlift.

Context stop: shared occupation geography does not make the film a depiction of Berlin.

1949 · 1 historical event

Alliances form; China changes course

NATO is founded as a collective-defence alliance. In China, Communist victory establishes the People's Republic while the Nationalist government retreats to Taiwan. The Soviet-led Warsaw Pact follows in 1955. These developments widen the rivalry, but communist states do not remain a single harmonious bloc: China and the Soviet Union will become bitter rivals.

Context stop: none of the films explains the Chinese Civil War or the later Sino-Soviet split.

1950 · 1 historical event

The Cold War becomes a hot war

North Korea invades South Korea. A US-led United Nations force intervenes, and China later enters on the North's side. The fighting devastates the peninsula; an armistice in 1953 stops major hostilities without a peace treaty. Calling this a proxy war must not erase Korean aims, decisions or suffering.

Context stop: no Korean War film is included in this short route.

A world beyond two camps

Asian and African states meet at Bandung to discuss sovereignty, anti-colonialism and cooperation. Their priorities cannot be reduced to choosing an American or Soviet patron. The conference helps prepare the ground for the later Non-Aligned Movement and is a reminder to read Cold War history from outside Europe as well.

Context stop: decolonisation and non-alignment have no direct screen representation in these selections.

Reform meets the limits of Soviet power

Khrushchev's denunciation of Stalin encourages hopes for change. In Hungary, an uprising challenges the communist government and Soviet domination; Soviet forces crush it in November. De-Stalinisation does not mean that Moscow will accept its allies leaving its orbit. The films' later East German settings belong to this wider history of coercion.

A border closes through Berlin

East German authorities, backed by the Soviet Union, close the sector border in Berlin to stop the flight of people to the West. Barbed wire gives way to a fortified wall. Bridge of Spies brings Donovan into this divided city; its dramatic encounters illustrate the danger of the border without documenting every incident it shows.

An exchange across the divide

Powers and Abel are exchanged at Glienicke Bridge; American student Frederic Pryor is released separately at Checkpoint Charlie. Bridge of Spies follows Donovan's efforts to secure both Americans' freedom. This is a real negotiated exchange, shaped into a drama with compressed chronology and invented scenes.

Decisions at the nuclear brink

Discovery of Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba produces a confrontation over inspections, a US naval quarantine and the risk of invasion. A settlement removes Soviet missiles from Cuba, includes an American pledge not to invade, and secretly provides for US missile removal from Turkey. Thirteen Days follows the American decision rooms; Cuban and Soviet perspectives need the accompanying history.

America escalates in Vietnam

The Gulf of Tonkin resolution gives President Johnson broad authority to expand US military action; large-scale bombing and American combat deployments follow. Vietnam's struggle also grows from colonial rule, partition and rival Vietnamese political projects. Read this before Full Metal Jacket, whose later combat story does not explain how the war began.

Tet challenges the story of progress

Communist forces launch attacks across South Vietnam during the Tet holiday. US and South Vietnamese forces retake lost ground, but the scale and visibility of the offensive damage American claims of progress. Full Metal Jacket follows fictional Marines into the fighting in Huế, offering a narrow, brutal ground-level perspective rather than an account of the whole offensive.

The Prague Spring is crushed

Alexander Dubček's reforms in Czechoslovakia raise hopes for a less repressive socialist system. A Soviet-led Warsaw Pact invasion ends the experiment. The Cold War includes conflicts within the communist world as well as confrontation across its borders; events in Prague are distinct from the simultaneous war in Vietnam.

Context stop: Full Metal Jacket shares the year, not the event.

1985 · 1 historical event

Reform and a different nuclear conversation

Mikhail Gorbachev takes power in 1985 and pursues economic restructuring and greater openness. Summits with Ronald Reagan lead to the 1987 Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty, eliminating a whole category of missiles. Reform and arms control change the possibilities, but the Soviet Union's eventual dissolution is not a settled plan.

Context stop: the films set in East Germany do not reconstruct Soviet reform or the Reagan–Gorbachev summits.

People bring the division to an end

Protest, emigration and changes across Eastern Europe undermine East Germany's leadership. On 9 November, a confused announcement brings crowds to the crossings and the Berlin Wall opens. One Germany, an uneven transition

Germany reunites after negotiations between the two German states and the four former occupying powers. Institutions, employment and everyday consumer life change quickly in the East. Good Bye Lenin! makes the disorientation personal, mixing affection, loss and satire; its family story is not a complete verdict on life under the GDR.

The Soviet Union dissolves

A failed hard-line coup in August weakens Gorbachev and accelerates the republics' moves towards independence. In December, the Soviet Union breaks apart and Gorbachev resigns. This is the guide's endpoint, two years after the Wall opened. Good Bye Lenin! ends its central story in 1990 and does not depict the Soviet collapse.

The rest of the picture

This is a short introduction through drama, weighted towards Europe and American viewpoints. Cold War rivalry intersected with local struggles, decolonisation and civil wars; those societies were participants with their own aims, not simply pieces moved by Washington and Moscow. Context stops cover China, Korea, Bandung, Hungary, Prague, Chile and Afghanistan, but no selected film directly tells those stories. African, Latin American, Soviet and Asian civilian perspectives, the Sino-Soviet split, the space race and many interventions deserve much more attention. Blank years are gaps in this guide, not years when nothing happened.

The Berlin Wall opened in November 1989, Germany reunited in October 1990, and the Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991. These were distinct developments, not one event. This guide ends in 1991, a conventional endpoint for the Cold War; nuclear arsenals, political divisions and the consequences of its wars remained. Films open a route into the history; the linked historical sources supply the wider picture.
