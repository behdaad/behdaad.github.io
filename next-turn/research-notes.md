# Next Turn — research notes

Research snapshot: 17 October 2026. SPIEL Essen: 22–25 October 2026.

Based on the supplied image of 22 games. Explicit objections to randomness and weak strategic agency outweigh conflicting star ratings. Blood Rage is the sole 5/5; Cosmic Encounter and Exploding Kittens each score 4/5, so the profile does not assume that all luck is unwelcome.

Fit is an editorial comparison, not a calibrated enjoyment prediction. Factors: control 30%, taste overlap 25%, replay potential 15%, group suitability 15%, approachability 15%; each factor is scored 1–5 and the total converted to 0–100. BGG average /10 and community weight /5 are independent, rounded, non-live snapshots. Some direct BGG fetches returned 403; readable search-index data and comments informed the review. Early scores can be unstable.

Prices and add-on listings were checked 17 October 2026. Euro references are preferred where verified; other currencies remain explicitly labeled without conversion. Each quote names its edition and language. Retail, MSRP, preorder and event offers are different kinds of reference; shipping and applicable checkout taxes may be extra. This is a sourced overview of verified official content, not an exhaustive list of third-party accessories or scarce promos.

## Six strongest established matches

### Kemet: Blood and Sand

Personal fit: **94/100** (High). BGG **7.9/10**, complexity **3.2/5**. 2–5 players; 60–180 minutes. Start with: 4–5; three also works.

Build a terrifying power engine, then fight for a reason. The closest match to your 5/5 Blood Rage experience.

Why it may fit:
- Your 5/5 Blood Rage result is the strongest signal: here you also grow from a modest army into a distinctive combination of powers and monsters.
- Buying visible power tiles lets you pursue a plan instead of waiting for an advantageous random hand.
- Conflict has strategic purpose: winning attacks and controlling temples move you toward victory, so passive turtling is rarely enough.

Caution: Direct attacks are constant. Divine Intervention cards and concealed combat choices can still surprise you; low dice luck does not mean an entirely predictable multiplayer board.

Randomness: No combat dice. Strength, chosen battle cards and purchased powers drive battles, with random Divine Intervention cards providing bounded surprises.

SPIEL: Demo not verified. This is an established-game recommendation. A specific playable SPIEL 2026 demo was not verified.

First-play question: After choosing one power upgrade and fighting twice, does losing a battle feel like a planning error you can learn from?

Review/preview evidence: A September 2026 comment by fskinner praises the evolving asymmetry through purchased tiles but finds combat too deterministic. That criticism is a positive fit signal for your stated preference, with the caveat that concealed intervention cards still matter. BaronEyeball's October 2026 comment praises two-, four- and five-player sessions and variety in the power development. This is one player's experience, not a consensus guarantee.

Edition note: Blood and Sand is the revised standalone game. Base game is enough; expansions are unnecessary for this recommendation.

### Hansa Teutonica: Big Box

Personal fit: **92/100** (High). BGG **8.4/10**, complexity **2.95/5**. 3–5 players; 45–90 minutes. Start with: 4–5; choose five when available.

Turn blocked routes into opportunities. A deeply interactive network game with very little random noise.

Why it may fit:
- Ticket to Ride's contested routes become a more interactive network puzzle with opportunities to improve your action economy.
- Opponents can displace your pieces, but you receive compensation. Reading their incentives and inviting the right obstruction becomes a skill.
- Simple turn options produce long-term development and many viable scoring routes, answering your desire for more than a greedy obvious move.

Caution: The theme and presentation are dry. It rewards reading opponents more than assembling flashy card combinations, and its openness can make the first game difficult to evaluate.

Randomness: No dice or shuffled action hand. Bonus markers introduce a little variation; most uncertainty comes from opponents' decisions.

SPIEL: Demo not verified. This is an established-game recommendation. A specific playable SPIEL 2026 demo was not verified.

First-play question: Can you see why allowing an opponent to bump one of your merchants may be better than completing your route immediately?

Review/preview evidence: Mystery McMysteryface's review emphasizes near-zero luck, planning and control; variability comes mainly from player interaction and bonus markers. October 2026 comments praise meaningful choices, several strategic paths, and the compensation when opponents disrupt your plans. Comments also warn that teaching displacement and bonus rules takes care.

Edition note: Big Box includes the base game and its expansion maps/modules. Begin on the main board without optional modules.

### Concordia

Personal fit: **90/100** (High). BGG **8.1/10**, complexity **2.98/5**. 2–5 players; 100 minutes. Start with: 3–4; Imperium map for 4–5, Italia for 2–4.

Choose your actions, build your economy, and make the map work for you. Planning stays firmly in your hands.

Why it may fit:
- Splendor's resource investment and Ticket to Ride's spatial competition meet in a game with longer plans and almost no output luck.
- You choose which action card to play from a known hand and when to recover your cards, so sequencing is under your control.
- New cards are both actions and scoring multipliers. Your economy, map position and final score pull in the same direction.

Caution: The experience is quieter than Blood Rage. Most scoring comes at the end, so the first game can obscure who is winning; use the rules' learning-game intermediate scoring.

Randomness: Random setup and market order vary opportunities. You do not draw a random action hand each turn and there are no combat dice.

SPIEL: Demo not verified. This is an established-game recommendation. A specific playable SPIEL 2026 demo was not verified.

First-play question: Does choosing when to recover your cards feel like a meaningful tempo decision rather than routine upkeep?

Review/preview evidence: The review praises how card actions, resource production, houses and scoring connect. It identifies the low randomness and limited direct attacks as potential drawbacks for players who want aggression. October 2026 comments praise clear play and the link between purchased cards and final scoring. A separate current rating comment dislikes having to mentally track scores, reinforcing the first-game scoring caveat.

Edition note: Base Concordia is sufficient. Concordia Venus is a standalone alternative with extra maps and team play for 4 or 6; do not confuse it with the Venus expansion requiring a base game.

### Inis

Personal fit: **87/100** (Medium–high). BGG **7.8/10**, complexity **2.95/5**. 2–4 players; 60–90 minutes. Start with: 3–4; two is a sharper duel.

A small draft, three ways to win, and a contest of timing. A strong match with a leader-bashing caveat.

Why it may fit:
- Drafting a small recurring pool of action cards gives you the reading-and-denial pleasure of Blood Rage with fewer sprawling rules.
- Three public victory conditions reward positioning, coexistence and timing; fighting is one tool rather than the only objective.
- Predictable core actions make opponents' decisions the central puzzle, while the modular island keeps the spatial game fresh.

Caution: The King Is Dead was only 3/5 for you, so elegant area control alone is not enough. Inis can involve leader-bashing, repeated near-wins and powerful Epic Tale surprises: try before buying if those features bother you.

Randomness: No battle dice; drafted action cards provide agency. Epic Tale draws and exploration tiles still create uncertainty, and multiplayer negotiation changes outcomes.

SPIEL: Demo not verified. This is an established-game recommendation. A specific playable SPIEL 2026 demo was not verified.

First-play question: Does holding a drafted action for the right moment feel satisfying, and do other players stopping your win feel tense or irritating?

Review/preview evidence: September 2026 comments praise fast drafting and intense back-and-forth interaction. One reviewer specifically prefers using the expansion's We Need a King module to control length. After many two-player plays, Matt Mueller emphasizes drafting and positional victory over ordinary conquest, while acknowledging Epic Tale and setup variance. His review used house rules, so it is not evidence that the standard game removes those risks.

Edition note: Base Inis is 2–4. A fifth player needs Seasons of Inis or an edition including it; the base-game rating is the one shown here.

### Dune: Imperium – Uprising

Personal fit: **85/100** (Medium–high; demo first). BGG **8.7/10**, complexity **3.53/5**. 1–4 or 6 players; 60–120 minutes. Start with: 4; three also works.

White Castle-style action planning meets a military race. Exciting, but hidden intrigue makes this a try-first pick.

Why it may fit:
- Cards force a meaningful tradeoff: use one to access a worker space now, or keep it for purchasing and combat later.
- Deck growth, faction influence and military preparation give several interlocking plans rather than a purely tactical fight.
- Its conflict rewards create reasons to watch every opponent, while spies give tools to work around occupied spaces.

Caution: This is the most luck-sensitive top-five pick. Shuffled hands, the market and secret intrigue cards matter; sandworms can multiply rewards into large swings. Its excellent BGG average does not cancel that mismatch risk.

Randomness: Deck draws and hidden intrigue can change the result despite strong preparation. Worker choices and spies provide mitigation, but it is not a low-luck deterministic Euro.

SPIEL: Demo not verified. This is an established-game recommendation. A specific playable SPIEL 2026 demo was not verified.

First-play question: Does losing a conflict to a hidden intrigue card feel like manageable risk, or the kind of swing that undermines your enjoyment?

Review/preview evidence: September 2026 comments include criticism of sandworm reward swings, even from players who like the underlying system. This is a real caution for your anti-chaos preference. Other October 2026 comments praise the deck-building/worker-placement tension, faction choices and deciding which battles to enter. An enthusiastic response to these strategic choices can coexist with discomfort about random swings.

Edition note: Uprising is a standalone game, despite occasionally being described as a standalone expansion. No original Dune: Imperium purchase is required.

### Foundations of Rome

Personal fit: **85/100** (Medium confidence). BGG **8.0/10**, complexity **2.02/5**, approximately 5,400 ratings in the accessible indexed snapshot. 2–4 players in the core game; five with the fifth-player expansion. 60–90 minutes. Start with: 3–4, core rules.

Contested spaces and clear economic choices echo Azul and Splendor. Lighter strategy than Blood Rage, with a much larger physical footprint.

Fit calculation: 20 × (4 × 0.30 control + 4 × 0.25 taste overlap + 4 × 0.15 replay potential + 4.5 × 0.15 group suitability + 5 × 0.15 approachability) = **85/100**. These dimension ratings are editorial judgments, not measured enjoyment probabilities.

- Control 4/5: visible market choices and spatial planning; shuffled deeds can frustrate land acquisition.
- Taste overlap 4/5: contested spaces and economic timing connect to Azul and Splendor; less power development than Blood Rage.
- Replay potential 4/5: changing land availability and opponents vary the puzzle; core strategic breadth is lighter than the leading recommendations.
- Group suitability 4.5/5: core support for your familiar 2–4 player counts, with five conditional on expansion; substantial table space.
- Approachability 5/5: three core turn actions and visible building choices make the initial decision structure accessible.

Why it may fit:
- As in Azul, the spaces you claim now change which placements remain possible later. The shared city gives opponents a direct role in your puzzle.
- Buying land, constructing buildings and taking income create an accessible timing puzzle that may appeal to your 4/5 Splendor and Splendor Duel ratings.
- Civic scoring can benefit from neighboring opponents. Reading their plans offers interaction beyond simply taking the largest immediate reward.

Caution: The core game offers less power development and strategic breadth than Blood Rage or The White Castle. Test whether its lighter decisions stay interesting after the spectacle wears off. The miniature city takes substantial table and storage space. Five-player support depends on the expansion; the physical production should not decide the fit score.

Randomness: Shuffled deeds determine starting lots and the order in which land reaches the public market. Core building and scoring have no dice checks. Visible prices and available lots let you plan purchases, but a useful neighboring deed can appear at the wrong time or be bought by an opponent.

SPIEL: Demo not verified. No specific playable SPIEL 2026 demo was established.

First-play question: After a few turns, can you explain when taking income or buying a neighboring lot is better than building for immediate points?

Review evidence: Accessible BGG comments praise easy teaching, the simple gameplay loop and the miniature city. Other comments question its strategic depth and whether presentation inflates its appeal. These are individual opinions, not a consensus; try the core game to judge the depth yourself.

Edition note: The core game supports 2–4. Five players requires the fifth-player expansion, included in Emperor and Maximus editions. Solo requires Gardens of Ceres and is excluded from this core-game recommendation. Statistics refer to the original 2022 BGG entry, not the separate Maximus entry. Begin without optional modules. Check the edition contents before arranging five players.
